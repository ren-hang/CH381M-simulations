How the lock-in gets Bevo out of the sunlight

The transmitter is chopped, so the wanted light is s(t) = A·c(t), with c(t) a 0/1 square wave at the chopper frequency. Everything the sensor also picks up — sunlight, drifting cloud shadow, lamp flicker, shot and Johnson noise — arrives as n(t), and here it is far larger than A. Look at the top trace: the beam is invisible in it.

v(t) = A·c(t) + n(t) → × r(t) = √2·sin(2πfchopt) and √2·cos(…) → low-pass τ → R = √(X² + Y²)

Multiplying by the reference shifts the wanted signal to DC and shifts the noise up around the chopper frequency. The low-pass filter of time constant τ then keeps only a narrow band around DC — noise bandwidth 1/(4τ) — so the noise that survives is a tiny slice of what came in. With a square-chopped beam and a sine reference the output settles near 0.45·A while the beam is clear (the rms of the chopper's fundamental) and falls to the noise floor the moment Bevo blocks it. The DC channel, a plain averaged photodiode with no chopping, has no such protection: drifting ambient light walks it straight through the threshold, which is exactly the false trip that makes a real garage door reverse on a sunny afternoon.

Two failure modes are worth provoking. Raise τ and the noise on the output shrinks as roughly 1/√τ, but the response time grows with τ — wind it up far enough and the door decides the Bevo has passed long after he has. And set the chopper to the lamp's 120 Hz: the interference now sits at DC after mixing, so the lock-in reads the lamp instead of the beam. Pick a chopper frequency nothing else in the room shares.
